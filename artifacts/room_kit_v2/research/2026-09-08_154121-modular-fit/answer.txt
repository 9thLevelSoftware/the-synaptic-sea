Yes. The central idea is: keep the joining surfaces exact, while adding detail away from those surfaces or through materials.

The clearest production source I found is Bethesda’s Skyrim/Fallout modular-kit breakdown. It explicitly says, “predetermine how thick floors should be,” and demonstrates tests for gaps, overlaps and incorrect corners.[7] Thickness is not inherently the problem.

How this applies to our isometric game

Our game uses real 3D geometry viewed through an orthographic camera. Orthographic projection changes how objects appear, not how their physical edges connect.[3] We should build an accurately fitting 3D kit—not distort or offset pieces to make one camera view look right.

1. Put floor thickness below a common walking surface.

My recommendation is one finished floor height and one slab thickness across compatible floor pieces:

  walking surface   -------------------------
                    | floor A | floor B |
  underside         -------------------------

Both slabs have depth, but their tops remain level. A repair plate should be recessed into that surface, not stacked above it. Door thresholds and grates should meet the same surface.

That is the crucial distinction: a thick floor is fine; a different finished height on each tile is not.

2. Use textures for detail that does not need actual depth.

Godot’s normal maps change how light reacts to a surface without changing its geometry.[4] Frozenbyte’s production guide recommends tiling textures and trim sheets for walls and floors, including baking detailed shapes onto flat surfaces.[8]

For our salvage-industrial style, I recommend:
  - Scratches, paint wear and hazard markings: color/roughness textures.
  - Small grooves, rivets and shallow panel seams: normal maps.
  - Repeated metal borders: a trim sheet—reusable surface artwork, not necessarily raised geometry.
  - Large braces, pipes and deep recesses: actual geometry, within allocated space.

This does not mean making everything flat. It means choosing which details genuinely need to change the shape.

3. Reserve space for wall thickness and decoration.

Bethesda distinguishes a module’s footprint from its usable interior space: walls and alcoves must fit inside the allocated footprint rather than intrude into adjacent modules.[7]

For our kit, wall panels, conduits and brackets should occupy a shared depth allowance. Wall ends, doorframes and corner pieces must share matching connection profiles. Simply thickening each asset independently is not sufficient.

Likewise, I would keep floor mating edges flat rather than beveling every tile into a separate raised-looking island.

4. Keep anchors fixed and test complete assemblies.

Bethesda establishes pivot conventions early and tests corridors that loop back into rooms. When a corner causes misalignment, its recommendation is to fix that corner’s footprint—not add a special patch-up piece.[7]

For us, that means preserving the existing 4 m grid and authoritative anchors, then testing mixed floors, doors, corners, rotations and damage variants together. No automatic recentering around decorative geometry; no per-piece nudges to disguise bad fit.

I would also check plain geometry and final materials separately, so a shading seam is not mistaken for an actual height mismatch.

One Godot caveat

Projected decals work in Forward+ and Mobile, but not Compatibility. For a Compatibility target, Godot suggests Sprite3D for mostly flat surfaces; baked material details are another option.[5] We should not make the kit depend on unsupported decal rendering.

Bottom line: the research supports your condition. Use a shared, precisely fitting structural shell; give it consistent thickness; then add recessed, material-based or envelope-limited detail. Zero thickness is not necessary to achieve clean joins.

These are research-backed recommendations, not a claim that our current assets have passed those assembly tests.
